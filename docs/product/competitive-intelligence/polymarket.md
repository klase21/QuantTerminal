## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is event/category driven. Hierarchy starts with event question, probability, price, volume/liquidity, and action. Grouping is by event category. Progressive disclosure moves from card to market detail. Density is moderate. Search and filtering support event discovery. Workspace is watchlist/event-monitor oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Cards are central. Probability charts and tables support market detail. Heatmaps are not core. Timelines can support event history. Network graphs are not relevant. Animations should be minimal. Typography is consumer-readable. Color language communicates yes/no probability and market movement.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions by reading market-implied probability, context, and price movement. Evidence is the market itself plus supporting context. Reasoning is mostly user-supplied. Historical comparison is limited to probability history. Confidence must not be confused with truth; liquidity/resolution rules matter. Counter-evidence comes from news/context outside the price.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is low. Onboarding benefits from plain-language event questions. Interaction is card, category, search, trade, watch, and chart driven. Customization is low-moderate. Productivity is good for event monitoring. Desktop and mobile both matter. Accessibility benefits from simple cards.

## 7. Strengths

- Event-card clarity.
- Probability as first-class product object.
- Strong category discovery.
- Approachable market framing.
- Useful for prediction evidence.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Probability can be mistaken for truth.
- Evidence context may be thin.
- Liquidity and resolution quality matter.
- Speculative tone can dominate.
