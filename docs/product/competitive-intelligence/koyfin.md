## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation centers on dashboards, watchlists, screeners, charts, market views, and asset pages. Hierarchy balances summary and depth. Grouping is asset-class and workflow driven. Progressive disclosure is strong: overview to chart to financials to comparison. Density is professional but readable. Search and filtering are important. Workspace is dashboard/watchlist oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Charts are polished and practical. Cards summarize markets and assets. Heatmaps support scanning. Timelines are less central but event/history context exists. Tables are strong for screeners and financial metrics. Network graphs are not core. Animations are minimal. Typography and spacing are modern and readable. Color language is restrained.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through comparative metrics, charts, dashboards, watchlists, and financial context. Evidence is mostly structured data. Reasoning is user-driven. Historical comparison is strong through charts and financial history. Confidence is implied through data quality rather than explicit. Counter-evidence can be assembled via comparisons.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is moderate. Onboarding benefits from familiar financial dashboard models. Interaction is search, dashboard, chart, screener, watchlist, and table driven. Customization is strong. Productivity is high on desktop. Mobile is secondary. Accessibility is helped by cleaner density.

## 7. Strengths

- Modern professional dashboard design.
- Strong watchlist and screener patterns.
- Balanced density.
- Cross-asset research orientation.
- Good bridge between retail and professional workflows.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Less crypto-native.
- Decision reasoning is mostly user-constructed.
- Some workflows may still require finance expertise.
- Evidence/source freshness is not always the headline.
