## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation centers on symbol search, charts, watchlists, screeners, ideas, and account tools. Hierarchy places the chart first. Grouping is workflow-driven: chart, indicators, drawing tools, alerts, panels, and community ideas. Progressive disclosure is strong because beginners can chart immediately while advanced users add indicators, scripts, layouts, and alerts. Search is excellent. Filtering is strongest in screeners. Workspace is saved-chart and layout oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Charts are the strongest pattern. Cards appear in market summaries and idea feeds. Heatmaps and tables support discovery. Timelines exist through events and news. Network graphs are not central. Animations are light. Typography supports interface density. Color language is familiar to traders and customizable.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through visual analysis, indicators, alerts, watchlists, and community ideas. Evidence is mostly chart/indicator based. Reasoning often comes from user-authored ideas rather than product-generated explanation. Historical comparison is chart-native. Confidence is not strongly source-governed. Counter-evidence depends on user construction.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is gentle at entry and deep at mastery. Onboarding benefits from immediate chart usability. Interaction is direct manipulation, toolbar, search, drag/drop, drawing, indicator, and alert driven. Customization is excellent. Productivity is high. Desktop is primary; mobile is strong for monitoring. Accessibility varies with dense chart interaction.

## 7. Strengths

- Visual-first charting.
- Low-friction entry.
- Excellent direct manipulation.
- Strong customization.
- Community distribution and discovery.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Community ideas can blur evidence and opinion.
- Source provenance is not always central.
- Technical-analysis bias can dominate.
- Decision support may become chart pattern narration without evidence hierarchy.
