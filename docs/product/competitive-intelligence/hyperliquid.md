## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is market/trading oriented. Hierarchy prioritizes chart, orderbook, order entry, positions, and market list. Grouping is execution-first. Progressive disclosure is limited because active traders need visible state. Density is high. Search/filtering focus on markets. Workspace is trading-layout oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Charts, orderbook, tables, position panels, and market lists dominate. Cards are minimal. Heatmaps may appear in market views but not core. Timelines are secondary. Network graphs are not relevant. Animations should be minimal and performance-focused. Typography and spacing favor rapid scanning. Color language communicates bid/ask, PnL, and market direction.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach decisions through live market state, chart, orderbook, positions, funding, and risk. Evidence is live-market data. Reasoning is user-owned. Historical context is limited. Confidence is not explicit. Warnings should focus on risk, margin, and execution.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is medium for traders and high for beginners. Onboarding must not interfere with speed. Interaction is click, keyboard, order entry, chart, and market selection. Customization is moderate. Productivity is high for trading. Desktop is primary; mobile supports monitoring/trading. Accessibility must balance speed and clarity.

## 7. Strengths

- High-performance trading workflow.
- Clear execution priority.
- Dense real-time layout.
- Fast market switching.
- Strong lesson for responsiveness.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Execution-first UX is not research-first.
- Little evidence narrative.
- High risk for beginner misunderstanding.
- Not a broad intelligence terminal.
