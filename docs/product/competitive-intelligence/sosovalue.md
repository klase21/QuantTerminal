## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is investment-category oriented: markets, ETFs, indexes, research/news, and asset pages. Hierarchy tends to place market/ETF summaries near the top. Grouping combines market data with narrative context. Progressive disclosure moves from summary cards to charts and detail pages. Density is medium-high. Search and filtering are asset/category oriented. Workspace is investor-monitor oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Charts and cards are central. Heatmaps may support market scan. Timelines appear through flow/news context. Tables support ETF and market comparisons. Network graphs are not central. Animations are secondary. Typography is modern dashboard style. Color language supports market direction and category emphasis.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through market summaries, ETF flow context, indexes, and research snippets. Evidence is partly data-backed and partly contextual. Reasoning is packaged but should be separated from source facts. Historical context appears through ETF/market charts. Confidence is not always explicit. Counter-evidence requires multiple widgets.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is moderate-low. Onboarding benefits from familiar investment dashboard structure. Interaction is card, chart, tab, search, and filter driven. Customization is moderate. Productivity is good for monitoring. Desktop is stronger than mobile for analysis. Accessibility depends on chart/card density.

## 7. Strengths

- ETF flow and crypto investment framing.
- Modern dashboard structure.
- Market context packaged for investors.
- Useful bridge between data and research.
- Good candidate for evidence-card inspiration.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Risk of blending data and narrative too tightly.
- Provenance and freshness must remain explicit.
- Can become dashboard-dense without clear first message.
- Less deep than institutional research platforms.
