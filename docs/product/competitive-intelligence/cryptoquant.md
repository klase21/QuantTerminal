## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is metric/category driven: exchange flows, miners, derivatives, network, and market indicators. Hierarchy often begins with charts and metric explanations. Grouping is data-source and indicator oriented. Progressive disclosure moves from chart to metric detail. Density is medium-high. Search/filtering depend on metric discovery. Workspace is chart/alert oriented.

## 4. Visualization

Charts dominate. Cards summarize indicators. Tables are secondary. Heatmaps may support overview states. Timelines exist through time-series indicators. Network graphs are not core. Animations are minimal. Typography supports chart interpretation. Color language highlights flows and thresholds.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through indicator interpretation and flow context. Evidence is metric-based. Reasoning can become signal-like if not bounded. Historical comparison is strong through indicator history. Confidence is often user-derived. Counter-evidence comes from comparing indicators.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is medium-high. Onboarding depends on metric education. Interaction is chart, metric, alert, and filter driven. Customization is moderate. Desktop is primary. Mobile is monitoring-oriented. Accessibility depends on chart clarity.

## 7. Strengths

- Strong on-chain/exchange-flow indicator library.
- Historical metric context.
- Useful market-cycle framing.
- Alerts and charts support monitoring.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Indicator interpretation can become overconfident.
- Metric taxonomy may be hard for beginners.
- Evidence and reasoning can blur.
- Less productized workflow beyond analytics.
