## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is product, data, research, index, and enterprise oriented. Hierarchy emphasizes data quality, analytics, and research. Grouping is institutional: market data, reference data, indices, analytics, research. Progressive disclosure is more sales/research oriented than app-workflow oriented. Search/filtering depend on data product surfaces. Workspace is not the public product focus.

## 4. Visualization

Charts and research visuals are strongest. Cards summarize products and research. Tables support data explanation. Heatmaps/timelines depend on research context. Network graphs are not core. Animations are minimal. Typography is professional. Color language is institutional and restrained.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through high-quality datasets, analytics, and research. Evidence is data-provider backed. Reasoning appears in research. Historical context is strong when datasets support it. Confidence comes from data quality/methodology. Counter-evidence may appear through research nuance and alternate metrics.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is moderate for professionals and high for retail users. Onboarding is data-product oriented. Interaction is less about dashboard UX and more about data access/research. Customization likely occurs through APIs and enterprise workflows. Desktop/research context is primary. Accessibility is professional-document oriented.

## 7. Strengths

- Institutional data trust.
- Methodology and data quality posture.
- Research-backed analytics.
- Strong enterprise positioning.
- Market microstructure relevance.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Less instructive for consumer app UX.
- Product surfaces may be less interactive.
- Enterprise/data orientation can feel abstract.
- Not a dashboard-first reference.
