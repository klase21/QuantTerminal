## 3. Information Architecture

Navigation is category-first: chains, protocols, stablecoins, yields, fees, bridges, and other data verticals. Hierarchy typically starts with headline totals, charts, then tables. Grouping is strong by crypto-native category. Progressive disclosure moves from ecosystem totals to protocol detail. Density is balanced. Search and filtering support protocol discovery. Workspace is not central.

## 4. Visualization

Charts show totals and trend lines. Cards summarize ecosystem numbers. Heatmaps are less central. Timelines appear through historical charts. Tables are the strongest component. Network graphs are not central. Animations are minimal. Typography is functional. Color language is simple and category-supportive.

## 5. Decision Support

Users reach conclusions through comparison: protocols, chains, categories, fees, yields, and changes over time. Evidence is mostly metric/table based. Reasoning is user-driven. Historical context is available through charts. Confidence is not a major explicit product layer. Warnings/counter-evidence require user interpretation.

## 6. UX

Learning curve is moderate-low for crypto users. Onboarding is mostly through obvious category navigation. Interaction is table, chart, filter, and detail-page driven. Customization is limited. Productivity is high for broad scanning. Desktop is primary. Mobile is usable for light checks. Accessibility is generally helped by simple structure.

## 7. Strengths

- Broad crypto-native data taxonomy.
- Clear category navigation.
- Simple, trusted, low-friction information access.
- Good protocol comparison patterns.
- Avoids overdesigned interfaces.

## 8. Weaknesses

- Limited visual storytelling.
- Limited workspace personalization.
- Decision support is mostly implicit.
- Evidence methodology can require extra research.
